01: The missing constraint

The next active learning decision is not only what to label. It is what is worth its cost.

Two examples can carry similar information while requiring very different annotation effort. A useful acquisition strategy must reason about both the value of a batch and the resources needed to obtain it.

The central question

Given a fixed annotation budget and non-uniform example costs, which subset provides the greatest expected information gain?

Uniform-cost active learning

Every label is treated as one identical unit. The method can select a highly informative batch that is too expensive in practice.

Cost-aware active learning

Each candidate has an informativeness score and an instance-level cost. Selection becomes a constrained optimization problem.

Overall cost-sensitive active learning objective
\[\max_{(B^1,\ldots,B^R)} \operatorname{Perf}(f^R) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{x\in B^r}g(x)\le C\]

The goal is to maximize final-model performance while respecting one global budget C across all R acquisition rounds.

Remaining budget after round r
\[C^r=C-\sum_{r'=1}^{r}\sum_{x\in B^{r'}}g(x)\]

Every acquired batch reduces the same global budget. The budget does not restart at each round.

KnapsackBALD batch construction
\[B^r=\underset{\bar A\subseteq A^k}{\operatorname{arg\,max}}\sum_{x\in\bar A}a(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{x\in\bar A}g(x)\le C^{r-1}\]

A k is the ranked candidate set, a(x) is informativeness, and the chosen subset becomes the round batch B r .

02: First, understand 0-1 knapsack

Each item is either selected once or not selected.

A 0-1 knapsack problem gives every item a value and a cost. For each item i, the binary variable z i is 1 when the item is selected and 0 when it is not. The goal is to maximize total value without exceeding the budget.

Generic 0-1 knapsack objective
\[\max_{z_1,\ldots,z_n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_i z_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{n}c_i z_i\le W, \qquad z_i\in\{0,1\}\]

v i is value, c i is cost, W is the budget, and z i is the keep-or-skip decision.

Dynamic-programming decision
\[DP(i,w)=\max\left(DP(i-1,w),\;v_i+DP(i-1,w-c_i)\right)\]

At each item and capacity, compare skipping the item with taking it. Taking is available only when the item fits.

04: The method

KnapsackBALD separates candidate generation, ranking, and constrained selection.

1 Preselect 80

BatchBALD identifies a diverse set of informative candidates from the unlabeled pool.

2 Rank and keep 60

HS keeps the most informative candidates. LC keeps the least costly candidates.

3 Solve 0-1 knapsack

The optimizer selects the highest-value combination that fits the remaining budget.

4 Repeat for 5 rounds

The batch is labeled, the model is updated, and the pool and budget are reduced.

The experiments use a global annotation budget equal to 30 percent of the normalized pool cost. Costs are normalized so their average is one, making results comparable across languages and cost scenarios.

05: Three ways costs become unequal

Non-uniform cost is not a single phenomenon.

Scenario 1 Varying costs

Examples receive heterogeneous costs directly, representing generic variation in annotation effort.

Scenario 2 Annotator reliability

More reliable annotation is modeled as more expensive, capturing a quality and cost tradeoff.

Scenario 3 Sample difficulty

Language-model perplexity represents difficulty. Harder examples receive higher annotation costs.

Each scenario is evaluated at baseline, moderate, and high levels of cost extremity. As the distribution becomes more uneven, ignoring cost becomes increasingly damaging.

07: What this changes

Counted labels are not the same as spent resources.

The experiments cover 13 MasakhaNEWS language conditions, including 11 African languages plus English and French, and use AfroXLMR-Large. Performance is measured after each acquisition round, with final results averaged across five random seeds.

Uniform-cost BatchBALD remains a strong baseline when costs are close to equal. As heterogeneity increases, cost-aware selection extracts more predictive value from the same annotation budget.

Limitation

The study simulates annotation costs.

The scenarios are controlled abstractions of real annotation markets. A natural next step is to estimate costs from observed annotation time, annotator expertise, disagreement, and language-specific access constraints.
