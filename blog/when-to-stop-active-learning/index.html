01 / The missing decision

Choosing the next example is only half the question.

In the earlier essays, I explored what happens around an acquisition decision: how to update without repeatedly relearning everything, how to account for unequal annotation costs, and how to collect evidence for the comparison we actually care about. The speech essay brought those questions to recordings and the people whose voices a model needs to understand.

Now imagine that another batch is ready. Someone must annotate it. A model must be updated. Evaluation must be repeated. The last few rounds helped, but each helped less than the one before.

Should we ask for more data, or stop? The answer depends on what remains uncertain, what improvement would change a decision, and what it would take to obtain that improvement. Spending the whole budget and reaching an acceptable model are different events.

Acquisition asks, “Which evidence next?”
Stopping asks, “Is more evidence worth obtaining?”

This is a research synthesis, not a report of a new stopping method or a new experiment from my papers. The numerical labs are deliberately constructed teaching examples. They let us inspect a decision whose future is usually hidden.

You can see the past.

01 / The false finish

Would you trust a plateau?

  1. Load the example and acquire four batches, one at a time.
  2. Read the last two gains. Would you stop?
  3. Stop, reveal the continuation, and compare the rules.
What to look for

A small recent gain describes the past. It cannot rule out improvements that require another region of the pool or several more batches.

02 / The hidden group

Whose progress counts?

  1. Load the example and run the recent-gain rule.
  2. Compare overall error with group B error.
  3. Reveal the comparison. What changes when both groups must meet a target?
What to look for

A group can remain poorly served while the overall curve looks flat. A group target also requires enough representative evaluation data.

03 / The resource limit

Does “finished” mean ready?

  1. Load the growing-cost example with 300 credits.
  2. Run the budget rule. Inspect the remaining money.
  3. Read the group errors and write down why acquisition ended.
What to look for

A positive balance may still be too small for the next complete batch. Record “next batch unaffordable” separately from “performance target met.”

What exactly is being simulated?

There is no trained model in this lab. Each scenario is a fixed table of error readings for two fictional groups, plus an independent uncertainty proxy. Overall error is 0.8 × group A error + 0.2 × group B error. The pool contains 600 additional examples, acquired in 12 batches of 50. Neither group represents a real accent, population, or dataset.

Annotation costs 40 credits per batch. Constant updates cost 10 credits; growing updates cost 10 + 2.5r credits at round r. These are invented accounting models, not measurements of FA or CF. Both share the same learning trace to isolate cost effects. A real change of update method can also change learning.

The recent-gain rule stops after two consecutive reductions strictly below 0.5 percentage points. The uncertainty rule stops at a proxy score ≤ 15. The group rule requires both displayed errors ≤ 12%. These thresholds are teaching choices, not validated recommendations. All rules obey the budget and pool limit. No intervals or measurement noise are simulated here; Experiment 05 examines uncertainty in an estimate.

Try to make a reasonable rule fail.

In A misleading plateau, the recent-gain rule sees little improvement before the trace improves again. Increasing patience could delay a stop, but hindsight cannot tell us what a practitioner knew at that moment. We would need evidence about why progress stalled.

In One group left behind, group A dominates the average and improves early. A small overall change can coexist with a large remaining group B error. The target rule asks a different question, and may run out of money before its requirement is met.

Finally, select the growing update cost. The same learning trajectory becomes more expensive. This connects back to the cost of repeated model updates: the learning curve alone does not describe the whole decision.

A signal is not a verdict

A stable model can still be wrong.

A stopping rule needs something observable. One possibility is the change in predictions on a fixed set of inputs. Bloodgood and Vijay-Shanker study this approach using chance-corrected agreement and a window of recent model comparisons. Such a stop set does not require reference labels, although it should reflect the inputs the model will encounter. Read their stopping-criterion paper.

Stability tells us that predictions have stopped changing. It does not, by itself, tell us that those predictions are correct. That distinction should feel familiar from the speech experiment, where repeated predictions can agree on the same omitted word.

A low uncertainty score is another possible signal, but its meaning depends on the model and the data on which it is measured. The remaining acquisition pool changes as examples leave it. A declining average can reflect that changing composition as well as learning. Compare like with like, and keep a representative evaluation view.

Even an apparent plateau deserves investigation. Laws and Schütze discuss stopping in named-entity recognition, including difficulties associated with relevant clusters that acquisition fails to discover. An exhausted strategy is not necessarily an exhausted learning opportunity. Read their analysis.

03 / Understand the uncertainty

Two predictions can average 50%.
For different reasons.

Imagine routing a support message to one of two teams. One plausible model gives team 1 a probability of 10%; another gives it 90%. The average is 50%, but the models disagree. Now imagine that every model gives 50%. The average is unchanged, yet each model is itself undecided.

These situations can suggest different investigations. Disagreement may point to missing training evidence. Shared indecision may reflect an ambiguous message, missing context, or a label definition that needs attention. Neither diagnosis follows from one score alone.

Epistemic uncertainty concerns what the model has not learned. Aleatoric uncertainty concerns variability or ambiguity under the information we observe. Better measurements can sometimes change that information. A third issue is evaluation uncertainty: how precisely a finite test sample measures performance. That is the question in the later interval lab. Kendall and Gal discuss the first two distinctions.

Work through the arithmetic

For binary probability p, entropy is H(p) = −p log₂(p) − (1−p) log₂(1−p), with 0 log₂(0) defined as zero. It ranges from 0 to 1 bit. Average the probabilities first to obtain p̄; then compare H(p̄) with the average of the individual entropies.

With 10% and 90%, the average is 50%: H(p̄) = 1 bit. Each individual entropy is about 0.469 bits, leaving 0.531 bits of disagreement. With two 50% predictions, both quantities are 1 bit, leaving zero. One pass always produces a difference of zero.

This entropy difference is a Monte Carlo form of the BALD information-gain objective when the predictions represent draws from a posterior over model parameters. Dropout can provide an approximate Bayesian interpretation under modeling assumptions. Arbitrary disagreeing classifiers do not automatically give a calibrated posterior. The within-pass term is conditional label uncertainty under the model, not a guaranteed measurement of irreducible real-world noise. See Houlsby and colleagues and Gal and Ghahramani.

Try this before deciding to acquire.

  1. Complete the disagreement pattern and note the two entropy terms.
  2. Switch to shared indecision and complete it. The mean probability is the same, but the difference is zero.
  3. Switch to confident agreement, complete it, and reveal the reference. The model can be confidently wrong.

More passes can help estimate a chosen uncertainty quantity. They do not add labeled training evidence, repair a shared blind spot, or certify accuracy. In this deterministic illustration the sequence is fixed, so it also does not demonstrate Monte Carlo convergence.

Connect this to stopping: the first lab's uncertainty proxy is deliberately independent of its error trace. This lab explains why treating that proxy as a direct probability of correctness would be a mistake.

04 / Count the work around a label

Asking where to learn
also costs compute.

Before annotating a batch, an active learner may score thousands of candidates. Ten stochastic passes through 4,000 candidates mean 40,000 candidate forward passes. After selection come annotation, training, and evaluation. Reducing training work does not remove the other stages.

The first experiment uses credits to keep the stopping decision simple. Here we open that accounting model. Compare updating on all acquired labels with updating only on the newest batch. Keep human effort in person-hours and compute in modeled GPU minutes. They are different resources and may overlap in calendar time.

For round r, remaining candidates are U₀ − (r−1)B. Scoring costs that count × T × 0.02 seconds. Full training costs (500 + rB) × E × 0.10 seconds; new-batch training costs B × E × 0.10 seconds. Evaluation costs 500 × 0.02 seconds per round. Annotation requires B × minutes-per-label.

Initial training and initial evaluation are sunk costs and excluded. The ledger scores the entire remaining pool each round. It omits selection-optimization overhead, storage, data loading, labeling the evaluation set, and variable sequence lengths. It assumes one GPU and linear effective rates. Real batching, hardware utilization, restart overhead, and update quality need measurement. Both strategies use the same scoring schedule only to isolate their training costs.

A worked first round.

At the defaults, scoring costs 4,000 × 10 × 0.02 = 800 seconds. Full training costs 600 × 3 × 0.10 = 180 seconds; new-batch training costs 100 × 3 × 0.10 = 30 seconds. Add 10 seconds for evaluation: totals are 16.5 and 14 minutes. Meanwhile, 100 labels at two minutes each require 3 hours 20 minutes of human effort.

Complete six rounds, then repeat with 30 scoring passes. The absolute training saving stays the same under these assumptions, but its fraction of total compute shrinks. Increasing the pass count scales scoring work; it does not establish a proportional improvement in acquisition quality.

This is why an efficient update method still needs an efficient acquisition procedure. Our full-versus-continual finetuning study investigates the update choice empirically. This ledger adds a separate teaching view of the surrounding work; it is not a reproduction of that paper's timings.

05 / Put the costs beside the gains

One percentage point can be worth very different amounts.

A small improvement matters more when it affects many decisions or avoids expensive mistakes. Cost-based stopping research makes the tradeoff between labels and future errors explicit. Pullar-Strecker and colleagues compare stopping criteria using such a cost measure; their results do not identify one rule that is best for every application. Read the study.

For intuition, suppose the next batch is expected to reduce the error rate by ΔR. If there are N future predictions and each avoided error is worth c credits, the estimated benefit is N × c × ΔR. Compare that with annotation and update costs.

Read the equation and its assumptions
V̂next = N × c × ΔR̂ − Cannotation − Cupdate

Convert percentage points to a fraction before multiplying: 0.5 pp is 0.005. The break-even reduction is total batch cost divided by N × c. Zero net value is a tie under this model, not evidence favoring another batch.

This is a one-step approximation with a fixed deployment horizon and equal value for every avoided error. It excludes discounting, distribution shift, unequal mistake costs, and improvements unlocked only by several batches. A negative estimate for the next batch does not prove that every longer acquisition plan has negative value.

A five-minute tutorial

Would better message routing pay for another batch?

Suppose 10,000 future messages will be routed, each avoided routing error is worth two fictional credits, and you estimate a 0.5 percentage-point error reduction. The expected benefit is 10,000 × 2 × 0.005 = 100 credits. A 200-credit batch gives a net estimate of −100.

The button changes only the value calculator above.

  1. Lower the batch cost to 50. The net estimate becomes +50 credits.
  2. Lower the estimated gain to 0.10 pp. The benefit becomes 20 credits and the net estimate returns to −30.
  3. State which uncertain assumption changed the decision. What observation would help you estimate it?

Do not insert the uncertainty score from the previous lab as the expected gain. Information gain, error reduction, and monetary value are different quantities. Estimate the conversion from comparable learning observations and make the remaining uncertainty explicit.

The difficult quantity is ΔR̂. We do not know tomorrow's gain simply because yesterday's gain is visible. Learning-curve extrapolation, validation observations, or a model of the acquisition process can inform an estimate, but they bring assumptions. Try a more pessimistic estimate and see whether your conclusion survives.

The connection to unequal annotation costs is direct: a promising batch can still be a poor use of the remaining resources. The connection to ranking-aligned evaluation is equally important: if the objective is selecting a model, value should reflect uncertainty in that selection, not an unrelated accuracy gain.

06 / Ask what the evidence supports

“The error is 6%” is an estimate.
How much did you measure?

If we stop because an error target appears satisfied, the size and composition of the evaluation sample matter. A point estimate can sit below a target while a plausible range still extends above it. A separate labeled evaluation set also has an annotation cost that belongs in the project budget.

Inspect a simple binary-error example. Keep the observed rate roughly fixed and change the sample size. Then decide whether the displayed interval sits entirely below your chosen target.

What this interval does and does not establish

The slider is converted to an integer error count, k = round(n × requested rate / 100). The actual estimate is k/n. The chart uses a two-sided 95% Wilson score interval with z = 1.96 for independent Bernoulli error observations at a fixed sample size.

For p̂ = k/n, the interval is [p̂ + z²/(2n) ± z√(p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/(4n²))] / (1 + z²/n). At zero observed errors, the upper endpoint remains above zero.

This calculation is for a binary error indicator, not WER, F1, or correlated observations. Repeatedly checking intervals and stopping when one crosses a target does not automatically preserve fixed-sample coverage. A sequential guarantee requires a method designed for repeated looks. Representative sampling and adequate evidence for relevant groups are separate requirements.

Read the interval yourself

What would make “6% error” convincing?

  1. Keep observed error at 6% and the target at 8%. With 50 examples, that is only three observed errors.
  2. Switch to 1,000 examples. The constructed rate is still 6%, now based on 60 errors. Compare the interval width and its upper endpoint.
  3. Set observed error to zero, then return to 50 examples. The interval still allows a nonzero error rate.

This exercise compares hypothetical samples. It does not add 950 real observations to your experiment. Before collecting more evaluation data, decide how the sample will represent the use case and whether repeated checking requires a sequential method.

A held-out set needs a role, too.

Use validation evidence to develop the stopping procedure. Keep a final test set separate from those choices. If the same held-out data repeatedly determine thresholds, model changes, and the stopping round, the resulting score is no longer an untouched final assessment. The reusable-holdout literature examines this broader problem of adaptive reuse. Read Dwork and colleagues.

Return to the speech question: if the overall result looks adequate but one accent remains poorly served, what exactly have we established? We would need appropriate group-level measurements and enough observations to interpret them. Merely adding a “worst group” number to a dashboard cannot create missing evidence.

07 / Use the ideas in a project

Three situations.
Three different next steps.

These are invented project scenarios. Use them to rehearse a decision; they are not reported deployments or performance results.

Text classification: the uncertain queue keeps growing

Situation. A support-message classifier has a large uncertain pool. Many messages say only “It still does not work.” All model passes remain close to 50%.

Inspect. Separate missing context from model disagreement. Check whether annotators can consistently choose a team from the same information the model receives.

Next step. Review a small representative sample with annotators. You may need conversation context or clearer routing rules before buying a large batch of ambiguous labels. Audit scoring costs before increasing the number of passes.

Stopping record. “Pause this acquisition strategy while we resolve the label definition.” This is different from declaring the classifier accurate enough.

Speech recognition: uncertainty falls, but omissions remain

Situation. A recognizer repeatedly drops the same word in recordings from a small speaker group. Its dropout transcripts agree.

Inspect. Compare recordings with reference transcripts from held-out speakers. Check omissions and group performance alongside the uncertainty signal.

Next step. Investigate examples the acquisition score misses. A targeted annotation pilot or a revised sampling strategy may be more useful than adding identical stochastic passes.

Stopping record. “Overall acquisition has plateaued; the group requirement remains unmet.” The Wilson example uses binary errors, so it is not directly a confidence interval for WER.

Model evaluation: the ranking is the decision

Situation. Two language models have nearly equal average scores, while the others are clearly separated. You have a limited evaluation budget.

Inspect. Ask which items can resolve that close comparison, whether benchmark coverage is adequate, and what ordering uncertainty remains.

Next step. Define an evaluation criterion tied to the intended comparison. The earlier RankBALD essay develops this change of objective. A generic training-error threshold does not answer the ranking question.

Stopping record. State which pairwise comparisons are resolved, which are still uncertain, and whether the remaining budget could alter the decision.

A small notebook habit worth keeping.

After every round, record the acquisition rule, labels added, annotation time, scoring time, update time, evaluation time, overall and group metrics, and the reason for continuing. Use a consistent evaluation protocol. Keep “ran out of resources,” “reached a target,” and “changed strategy” as separate outcomes.

Try the compute arithmetic in Python

This dependency-free example reproduces the default ledger. Replace the illustrative per-example rates with measurements from your own hardware and batching setup. It estimates work, not learning gains or deployment readiness.

pool, labeled, batch, passes, epochs = 4000, 500, 100, 10, 3
for round_id in range(1, 7):
    scoring = pool * passes * 0.02
    full_update = (labeled + batch) * epochs * 0.10
    new_batch_update = batch * epochs * 0.10
    evaluation = 500 * 0.02
    print(round_id,
          (scoring + full_update + evaluation) / 60,
          (scoring + new_batch_update + evaluation) / 60)
    pool -= batch
    labeled += batch

Columns: round, full-update total GPU minutes, new-batch-update total GPU minutes. At round 1 the values are 16.5 and 14.0. Annotation is separate: batch × minutes per label.

08 / Close the loop without closing the question

Stopping acquisition is a decision.
It is not a certificate of readiness.

A budget can force a stop while performance is inadequate. A plateau can suggest reviewing the acquisition strategy or representation. A well-supported target can justify ending a particular experiment while leaving monitoring and future adaptation necessary.

The useful distinction is between stop collecting this way, stop this learning run, and accept this model for a defined use. Each requires a different argument. If the evidence does not support the use, reaching the budget limit does not make that evidence stronger.

Before acquisition

Define the decision, the relevant groups, the resource limit, and the evidence you will inspect.

During acquisition

Track costs and comparable performance readings. Investigate unexpected plateaus rather than treating every flat curve as completion.

At the stopping point

Record why you stopped, which requirements remain unmet, and what new evidence would make you reopen the decision.

The question that connects these essays is how to spend learning effort well. Sometimes that means acquiring a different batch. Sometimes it means changing the update rule or the evaluation objective. And sometimes it means recognizing that the next useful step is not another label.

A thoughtful learner needs a reason to ask.
It also needs a reason to stop.
