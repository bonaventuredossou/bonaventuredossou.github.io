01: The data problem

If you can label only 1,000 examples, which 1,000 should they be?

Machine learning often starts from an asymmetry: unlabeled data can be plentiful, but useful labels are expensive. They may require a fluent speaker, a clinician, a scientist, or simply time that a team does not have.

Random sampling spends the annotation budget without asking whether one sample is more useful than another. Active learning makes a different bet: score the unlabeled samples according to their expected usefulness, acquire a small batch of the most informative samples, annotate that batch, and then update the model.

The central decision

Active learning asks which individually scored samples should form the next acquisition batch . Samples receive separate uncertainty scores, but the model update happens only after the complete batch has been selected and annotated.

02: The acquisition signal

How do we identify the samples that belong in the next batch?

A common answer is uncertainty sampling. In binary classification, a prediction of 50/50 is less certain than a prediction of 99/1. Predictive entropy turns that intuition into a score:

\[ H[y\mid x] = -\sum_{c=1}^{C} p(y=c\mid x) \log p(y=c\mid x). \]

Entropy is largest when probability is spread evenly across classes and shrinks as one class dominates.

But uncertainty has more than one source

A sample can be difficult because the observation or label is genuinely ambiguous. This is often called aleatoric uncertainty . It can also be difficult because the model has not learned enough yet. This is epistemic uncertainty . Additional informative data can reduce the second kind.

≈

Aleatoric

The plausible models are similarly unsure. The ambiguity is in the observation or its label.

≠

Epistemic

The plausible models disagree. The uncertainty reflects knowledge that additional data may help resolve.

Approximate uncertainty with MC Dropout

A conventional neural network is usually treated as though one fitted parameter vector, \(\theta^*\), is the answer. A Bayesian view instead reasons over plausible parameters after observing data, \(p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)\), and averages their predictions:

\[ p(y\mid x,\mathcal D) = \int p(y\mid x,\theta) p(\theta\mid\mathcal D) d\theta. \]

The variation across plausible models indicates where the model's knowledge remains fragile.

For deep networks, the posterior is difficult to compute exactly. Monte Carlo Dropout offers a practical approximation. Dropout remains enabled at inference time, and the same sample passes through the network repeatedly. Each dropout mask produces a different stochastic prediction.

The acquisition procedure used in the paper

At every active learning round, we perform 10 stochastic forward passes with dropout enabled at inference time. We compute the average token-level entropy separately for every sample in the unlabeled pool \(U\).

We then select the top 100 most uncertain samples . These samples collectively form the newly acquired batch \(Q'_r\). After the batch is labeled, it is added to the training set. The model is updated once for that round.

Sample-wise selection, batch-wise update

Uncertainty is calculated for each sample. The 100 highest-scoring samples form one acquisition batch. We do not update the model after each individual sample is scored or selected.

Deeper dive Where does BALD fit?

Predictive entropy and BALD are related, but they are not identical. Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement targets the information that a sample carries about the model parameters:

\[ I(y;\theta\mid x,\mathcal D) = H[y\mid x,\mathcal D] - \mathbb E_{p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)} H[y\mid x,\theta]. \]

The first term asks whether the aggregate prediction is uncertain. The second asks whether each plausible model is uncertain on its own. Their difference becomes large when the stochastic models are individually confident but disagree with one another.

Important distinction: the experiments in this paper use uncertainty sampling with MC Dropout and average token-level entropy. They do not use BALD as the acquisition function. BALD becomes central in the later essays on KnapsackBALD and RankBALD.

03: The hidden cost

We saved labels. Then we trained on everything again.

Most active learning work focuses on the cost of annotation. Yet every acquisition round also updates the model, and the standard update becomes more expensive as the labeled set grows.

The acquisition procedure gives us a new batch \(Q'_r\) containing the 100 samples selected during round \(r\). Once that batch is labeled, we must decide how to use it to update the model.

Should the model return to the original checkpoint and relearn from all labeled data, or should it continue from its current state using only the newly acquired batch?

Our paper compares two update strategies:

FA

Finetuning All

Return to the original pretrained checkpoint after every acquisition round and retrain on the full accumulated labeled dataset.

CF

Continual Finetuning

Keep the model produced by the previous round and update it using only the most recently acquired batch \(Q'_r\).

\[ \text{FA: } \theta_r = \operatorname{FT} \left( \theta_0, D_{\mathrm{train}}^{(r)} \right) \]
\[ \text{CF: } \theta_r = \operatorname{FT} \left( \theta_{r-1}, Q'_r \right) \]

FA restarts from the original pretrained checkpoint \(\theta_0\) and uses all accumulated labeled data. CF continues from \(\theta_{r-1}\) and uses only the latest acquired batch.

04: What we found

Continual finetuning cuts compute, but the answer is not simply “always use CF.”

Finding 1: the resource reduction is substantial

Across languages, CF reduced GPU memory, FLOPs, and wall-clock training time by roughly one-third or more. Select the dataset to inspect the measured average reductions.

Finding 2: pretraining coverage and linguistic proximity matter

CF most consistently matched or outperformed FA for languages represented during AfroXLMR's pretraining. But pretraining was not a binary gate. Several unseen Bantu languages also benefited, suggesting that related representations can make small, targeted updates effective.

Finding 3: continual updates depend on principled acquisition

When we replaced uncertainty-based selection with random acquisition, CF deteriorated across languages. This matters because CF sees only the latest acquired batch. If that batch is redundant or uninformative, there is no accumulated dataset in the update to compensate.

What this does not mean

Continual finetuning is not a universal replacement for full finetuning.

FA can remain preferable when pretraining alignment is weak, learning dynamics are unstable, scripts or structures are poorly represented, or the data distribution is highly imbalanced. The scientific question is not which strategy wins globally. It is what evidence should make the system switch between them?

The broader lesson: active learning is a system, not only an acquisition rule.

We began with a label budget. But the full system also contains sample-wise uncertainty estimation, batch acquisition, model updates, compute, pretraining coverage, and the structure of the domain. Optimizing one piece while treating the others as free can move the cost rather than remove it.

Score

Estimate uncertainty separately for every sample.

Acquire

Form the next batch from the top 100 samples.

Update

Decide how much previously labeled data the model must revisit.

Adapt

Change update strategies when the evidence requires it.
