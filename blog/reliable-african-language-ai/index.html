Reliable language AI for African contexts

When does language coverage become reliable support?

A fluent answer can still send someone to the wrong place, on the wrong day. I trace what it takes to turn language coverage into a service people can depend on.

By Bonaventure F. P. Dossou

I wrote this essay for linguists, language practitioners, and AI researchers interested in how language technologies are evaluated and used. I connect the learning process to questions about meaning, variation, interpretation, and who defines a successful interaction.

A short glossary for the machine-learning terms
NLP and LLM
Natural language processing is the field of computational methods for language. A large language model is a model trained on large amounts of language data to predict and generate sequences of tokens.
Tokens
The units a model processes, often words or parts of words. They need not correspond to linguistic morphemes.
Grounding
Supporting an answer with relevant evidence. Agreement with an outdated or inapplicable source does not establish present accuracy.
Calibration
Agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes under a specified evaluation criterion. It depends on how those outcomes are defined and annotated.
Epistemic uncertainty
Uncertainty associated with limited knowledge in the model. More informative evidence may reduce it, but an estimate does not diagnose every cause of error.
Acquisition function
The rule that ranks candidate examples for annotation or evaluation. Its objective determines what evidence is prioritized.
Fine-tuning
Updating a previously trained model using additional examples for a task or setting.
Distribution shift
A change between the data used to develop or evaluate a system and the inputs it encounters in use.
FLOPs
Floating-point operations: a count of numerical computation. It is not, by itself, elapsed time, energy use, or total cost.
Answer coverage
The proportion of questions the system answers rather than defers. Here it differs from language coverage, which concerns the languages a system claims to support.

01 / From inclusion to dependable use

The answer sounds right.
The registration deadline is wrong.

Imagine someone asking a community learning centre: “Can I register on Friday for the Saturday class?” The assistant replies yes. Its answer is clear and natural. The centre’s notice, however, says registration closes on Thursday at 17:00. Someone who follows the answer could miss the deadline.

This is an illustrative failure, but it gives the reliability question a concrete form. Recognizing the language and producing a fluent response are only part of the task. The assistant must understand the question, find the relevant notice, distinguish the registration deadline from the class time, and respond appropriately if any of that evidence is missing.

In a multilingual service, each step needs attention. A voice message may include an accented place name, a local course name, or code-switching. An official notice may use a different language or spelling convention from the person asking. I would want to know whether the system preserves the meaning through those changes, and whose questions are missing from the evaluation.

These are the questions connecting my work on uncertainty, active learning, and data-efficient adaptation. Reliability means useful performance under specified conditions, evidence that supports the claim, and an appropriate response when those conditions are not met.

Broader representation makes this question more urgent.

AfroBench brings together 64 African languages, 15 tasks, and 22 datasets. IrokoBench contributes human-translated evaluation across 17 African languages and three tasks. Both expand the evidence available to researchers. Neither benchmark, on its own, establishes that this registration assistant serves its intended users reliably.

“African contexts” is deliberately plural. Languages differ in resources, writing conventions, and patterns of use. A country is not a language group, and a language label does not identify every speaker’s variety. I would define the intended setting with speakers and domain experts, including which failures matter most to them.

A documented linguistic example / Yorùbá

Removing a mark can remove a distinction.

In their study of Yorùbá and Twi representations, Alabi and colleagues (2020, Section 3) give Yorùbá words whose meanings differ with their diacritics. Three of their examples are shown below. These are published lexical examples, separate from the fictional registration service.

owómoney
òwòbusiness
ọwọ́hand

The written forms preserve distinctions in tone and vowel quality.

If a preprocessing step strips every combining mark, all three forms become owo. The display demonstrates that transformation; it is not a measured model error. Context may help recover an intended meaning, but the isolated stripped form no longer preserves the distinction.

For an assistant, my concern is the interpretation passed to retrieval and generation. A payment question and a reference to a hand require different evidence. A silent, incorrect restoration could send the system toward the wrong topic even if the final answer is fluent. That is a possible downstream consequence, not an incident reported by this paper.

I would preserve the original input, evaluate the preprocessing choices with competent speakers, and include naturally occurring marked and unmarked text in the audit. If the context leaves a consequential ambiguity, clarification is more defensible than silently selecting a meaning. This is one Yorùbá example, not a rule for every African language.

Language in use / Define what should be preserved

Reliable for which speakers and communicative purposes?

The Yorùbá example concerns a lexical distinction, but reliability also involves how an utterance is used in context. In the registration question, “can” could concern permission, practical possibility, or an available procedure. In this scenario, the notice makes the deadline relevant. In another conversation, the assistant might need to ask which interpretation the person intends.

Variation belongs in the task definition. Piergallini and colleagues’ work on Swahili–English code-switching studies language identification within mixed-language data. For this service, I would ask whether switching languages preserves the intended question and whether the answer remains useful. I would not make conformity to monolingual edited prose a condition for receiving a correct response.

Likewise, accent, dialect, register, and recording quality call for different analyses. The “noisy speech” category in the labs concerns audio conditions, not an assessment of a speaker’s variety. The Yorùbá demonstration concerns the loss of written distinctions, not a judgment about the competence of someone writing without marks. These distinctions affect which data is collected and what counts as an error.

Follow the same question through the system.

I use the learning-centre assistant throughout this essay. First, trace the Friday question from input to response. Then inspect who appears in its audit, which drafts it releases, and how it allocates review effort. These are connected decisions: a higher average or fewer labels means little if the system still fails the people it claims to serve.

Illustrative service. The walkthrough and labs use authored cases and synthetic values. Research findings are cited separately.

How to interpret the examples

The English questions are teaching examples, not collected translations or claims about any particular language. No audio is played or transcribed, no live model is queried, and no training is performed. Scores, review times, and audit counts do not measure a real community or deployed service. The labs expose different decisions in the same fictional service; their cases are separate pools, not a longitudinal experiment.

02 / Inspect the evidence behind the average

A good average can rest on a narrow audit.

Now evaluate the assistant from the walkthrough. Suppose most test questions are short, single-language text messages. The reported average will say much more about those messages than about noisy speech or code-switching. More test cases help only if their distribution answers the question being asked.

I would report the number of reviewed cases, performance, and uncertainty for each relevant context. I would also distinguish the audit’s composition from the intended traffic distribution. The traffic-weighted estimate is the sum of each context’s pass rate multiplied by its assumed traffic share. It is meaningful only if the shares reflect the intended use and the within-context audits are representative. A balanced audit is useful for comparing contexts; its unweighted average need not estimate deployment performance.

What these intervals do and do not capture

The Wilson intervals illustrate uncertainty from finite binomial samples. They do not account for duplicated conversations, reviewer errors, missing groups, or distribution shift. The weighting example likewise does not remove sampling bias within a context.

From linguistic analysis to an evaluation rubric

What should count as an acceptable answer?

The audit’s pass/fail label compresses several judgments. For the registration service, I would examine meaning and reference, support from the notice, relevance to the person’s question, and suitability for the interaction. A grammatical sentence can fail the task; two differently worded answers can both succeed.

Authored English examples, using the same Thursday deadline. Open each draft to inspect the reasoning.

Draft 1 “No. Registration closes on Thursday at 17:00.”

This answers the Friday question and supplies the relevant deadline. The directness of “No” is a separate matter for assessment in the intended interaction; factual support does not settle every question of register or interpersonal appropriateness.

Draft 2 “Friday would be too late. Please register by Thursday at 17:00.”

This expresses the same deadline and makes the practical consequence explicit. A rubric should allow this paraphrase rather than requiring the wording of Draft 1. I would assess suitability with the intended users instead of imposing a universal preference between the two.

Draft 3 “The class begins on Saturday at 09:00.”

The statement is supported by the notice, but it leaves the registration question unanswered. A check for factual consistency alone would miss the conversational failure.

I would establish acceptable alternatives and consequential errors with speakers, linguists, and service staff. In translation or transcription, a single reference may likewise leave legitimate alternatives unrepresented. The evaluation should specify which distinctions matter for the task and retain examples that expose the limits of the rubric.

03 / Make uncertainty useful

Fluency is not evidence of correctness.

A language model can produce a natural sentence even when it has misunderstood a local expression or lacks the relevant source. I would not interpret “I am confident” as a calibrated probability. Token likelihood also measures a different event from whether an entire answer is correct and supported.

Calibration connects scores to observed outcomes: among comparable predictions assigned about 80% probability of being correct, approximately 80% should be correct. Guo and colleagues study this distinction for neural classifiers. It is a useful starting point, not evidence that a prompted confidence statement from an LLM has the same property.

In a multilingual service, I would check that relationship separately where there are enough labels to do so. Good aggregate calibration can hide differences across contexts. Calibration estimated on edited text also need not survive a move to spontaneous speech. Ovadia and colleagues’ study of dataset shift shows why uncertainty estimates themselves require evaluation.

Calibration also depends on the judgment being predicted.

Baan and colleagues (2022) show why calibration against a majority label can be problematic when human judgments inherently vary. For this essay’s deadline example, contradiction with the current notice is a comparatively clear criterion. Ratings of conversational adequacy may require several judgments and their context. I would specify that target before interpreting a confidence score.

These four drafts are a constructed counterexample, not samples from a live model. The count measures agreement on this one claim; it is not a BALD score or a calibrated correctness probability.

What I would change in the learning loop

I would combine uncertainty-based acquisition with source checks and a representative random audit. Confident errors found in that audit can motivate new development examples, but I would then reserve fresh evidence for the next final evaluation. Repeated model calls alone do not provide independent verification.

Uncertainty about what?

Three different evidence gaps appear in this example. The model may be wrong about one answer. An evaluator may have too few reviewed cases to estimate performance precisely. Or the required fact may be absent from the source. Treating all three as a low confidence score would hide what needs to happen next.

Within predictive uncertainty, missing training experience may contribute epistemic uncertainty; an ambiguous recording may remain difficult even for a well-trained model. Additional labels can help with the first, while a clearer recording may be the immediate response to the second. A single score rarely establishes which explanation is correct.

A missing registration policy is a different problem again. If no current notice exists, more confident generation cannot establish the deadline. I would ask the centre to supply the fact, and give the person a usable route to staff. Uncertainty quantification helps identify where evidence needs attention; it does not supply that evidence by itself.

An uncertainty estimate should change the response.

For the centre’s assistant, useful uncertainty might trigger a focused clarification, a request for clearer audio, or a referral to a staff member. The fallback should be available in a language and format the person can use. A system that defers every difficult case can look accurate while providing little service.

Reviewer judgments are used only to calculate the displayed metrics. The decision rule sees the score, not the judgment. The exercise changes a threshold; it does not train or improve a model.

Look beyond the overall error rate.

Start at 0.80 and inspect the coverage bars: no noisy-speech case is answered. Then try 0.95. A high-scoring error remains, so a stricter threshold does not necessarily lower the observed error fraction. Finally, set 1.00. Answering nothing produces an undefined error rate among answers, not proof of a reliable service.

I would choose and validate a threshold before using an untouched test set. Then I would report answer coverage, errors among released answers, unnecessary deferrals, and the usefulness of the fallback, including results by context. Selective question answering under domain shift studies this relationship between answering and abstaining. A threshold calibrated in one setting is not a guarantee in another.

A referral should lead somewhere.

The threshold exercise counts which answers the assistant withholds. I would also ask what happens to those people. Does a qualified reviewer respond before the registration deadline? Does the review resolve the question? How much work does the fallback create?

04 / Spend annotation effort deliberately

Which examples deserve the next human review?

If a language has few reviewed examples, each annotation may involve finding a qualified speaker, resolving a transcription, and checking local usage. The cost is not captured by counting labels alone. Time, reviewer expertise, compensation, and adjudication all matter.

The review stage of the walkthrough produces a concrete learning opportunity. If Friday was misheard, a reviewer can supply the transcription. If the notice was retrieved correctly but contradicted, a reviewer can label the unsupported claim and provide a grounded answer. If the notice was outdated, the first correction belongs in the source collection. Active learning helps decide which candidate cases deserve scarce annotation effort.

In my study of African-accented English speech recognition, I use uncertainty to guide adaptation. Across the study’s evaluated settings, the paper reports a 27% relative average reduction in word error rate and about 45% less data than its baselines. Those are results for the paper’s speech experiments, not a forecast for this assistant. African-accented English also differs from speech recognition in African languages.

The connection is methodological: concentrate review on examples that expose model weaknesses, then test whether the update helps beyond those examples. My speech essay includes real AfriSpeech recordings alongside explicitly simulated predictions, so readers can explore that distinction directly.

For the learning-centre assistant, I would consider uncertain drafts, uncovered service cases, repeated failure patterns, and annotation cost. Selecting only uncertain examples can repeatedly return one familiar failure type. Confident mistakes may never be selected. A coverage requirement and some random review can help investigate those blind spots, but their effect still needs to be measured.

A disagreement can reveal a problem in the rubric.

Plank’s discussion of human label variation explains why annotation differences cannot always be treated as noise. In this service, I would distinguish an annotator overlooking the deadline from two reviewers reasonably differing about an indirect answer’s usefulness.

This matters for active learning. An uncertainty-based rule can repeatedly select an ambiguous construction. More labels may document several defensible readings rather than establish one uniquely correct label. I would record the reasons for disagreement and whether additional conversational context resolves it, instead of assuming that majority vote is always the appropriate training target.

By linguistic expertise, I mean familiarity with the relevant varieties, practices, and task. One speaker need not represent an entire named language. I would include appropriate perspectives, compensate review work, and document how contested judgments are handled. Participatory research in African-language machine translation provides a concrete precedent for involving contributors in research beyond supplying examples.

What does this selection optimize?

The first rule solves a small cost-constrained subset problem, maximizing the sum of the supplied values. The coverage rule adds a requirement to include all four contexts. Neither value is a measured learning gain, and summing them does not model redundancy. The random rule uses a fixed shuffle and takes candidates that fit the remaining budget.

A real acquisition function might use posterior disagreement or estimated information gain. Its training benefit must be assessed on separate evaluation data. This exercise demonstrates allocation and coverage, not a trained model or a performance advantage.

Try the coverage constraint.

  1. At 12 minutes, select the highest-value batch and inspect which contexts it represents.
  2. Require all four contexts and select again. Observe the change in allocation and acquisition value.
  3. Lower the budget to six minutes. The constraint is infeasible. A truthful system should say so instead of silently dropping a context.

Coverage across four coarse contexts is not coverage across every language, dialect, or subgroup. I would choose those dimensions with speakers and domain experts, then examine their intersections where sample sizes permit. This is a design decision, not something an uncertainty score can settle.

Separate learning from auditing.

The selected reviews can improve the assistant or its evaluator. They should not also serve as an unbiased estimate of its performance. Reviewing unusually difficult cases changes the distribution being measured. Active testing addresses selection bias when estimating performance; active learning serves the different objective of improving the model.

Choosing evidence for a ranking is another objective.

In RankBALD, my work with Jackie Chi Kit Cheung, evaluation items are selected for what they reveal about pairwise model ordering. The LLMs remain fixed. At 100 items per benchmark, Table 1 reports rank distance of 24.23 for RankBALD and 36.25 for Fluid, a 33.2% relative reduction. This measures ranking recovery, not answer accuracy or hallucination reduction.

For the centre, that distinction matters when choosing an assistant. Efficiently identifying which model ranks higher does not show that either model handles every language variety well. RankBALD has no explicit domain or subgroup coverage constraint, and its current method assumes pre-fitted item parameters. I would investigate coverage constraints alongside an independent audit before extending that ranking claim to service reliability.

I would reserve a separate audit set, keep it out of acquisition and tuning, and compare coverage-constrained, unconstrained, and random selection at matched total effort. Repeatedly changing the model after inspecting that audit eventually turns it into development data. A final claim then needs fresh independent evidence.

05 / Data efficiency is a system property

Useful learning includes the cost of getting it right.

More data can help. So can correcting a small set of systematic errors, removing duplication, improving the task definition, or providing a current source that the model did not have. Data efficiency asks which of these changes delivers a useful improvement for the effort available.

For this assistant, I would first check whether retrieval supplies the correct notice. If it does not, a more confident answer generator is not the immediate remedy. I would then examine transcription, language identification, and answer generation separately, while still evaluating the end-to-end response that reaches the user.

My work with Ines Arous and Jackie Chi Kit Cheung compares continual updates on newly acquired examples with full fine-tuning on all acquired data. Across 28 African languages in MasakhaNEWS and SIB-200, the findings depend on pretraining coverage and acquisition strategy. For languages included in pretraining, the paper reports comparable or better performance with reductions of up to 35% in GPU memory, FLOPs, and training time. Full fine-tuning remains preferable in some settings.

That result changes how I would budget the assistant’s learning loop. Annotation, uncertainty estimation, model updates, and re-evaluation each consume resources. Reusing a checkpoint may make another round affordable, but I would still test for regressions in the languages and conditions already served. The cheapest update is useful only if it preserves the required quality.

I would record annotation minutes, reviewer disagreements, pool-scoring cost, update cost, and service quality. Any claim of efficiency should specify the baseline and the quality being held constant. Fewer annotations alone do not establish lower total cost or more reliable behavior.

Language expertise is part of this system. Participatory research on African-language machine translation provides an important precedent for involving language communities in the research process. For a service, that involvement should shape the task, acceptable answers, data permissions, compensation, and correction process. Resource scarcity also reflects decisions about investment and participation; it is not a property of a language’s value or complexity.

06 / Turn “ready” into an evidence claim

What would justify putting the system into use?

I would begin with a bounded service rather than an unrestricted claim of language support. For the centre’s assistant, that means specified notices, supported input types, intended users, an answer rubric, and a human route when the evidence is missing.

  1. Define the claim with the community. State which tasks, varieties, input conditions, and failure consequences the evaluation covers.
  2. Build distinct evidence sets. Use development data for improvement, validation for choices such as the answer threshold, and an independent audit for the final claim. Keep related speakers or conversations together when splitting data.
  3. Measure performance and access to service. Report sample counts, uncertainty intervals, errors, answer coverage, and appropriate fallbacks across the relevant contexts.
  4. Compare at matched effort. Include random selection, annotation time, and the cost of scoring, adaptation, and auditing.
  5. Limit and monitor deployment. Track new failure types, source changes, and shifts in usage. Define who reviews them and what would trigger a rollback or a narrower scope.

For the Friday question, a corrected answer closes one incident. The final stage of the walkthrough asks the harder question: does the proposed change also work on other dates, course names, voices, and language varieties? Only a separate evaluation can support that claim.

Some groups will have too little evidence for a confident estimate. I would report that gap explicitly and plan how to address it, rather than borrowing the certainty of a larger group. A model’s uncertainty about an answer and an evaluator’s uncertainty about a performance estimate are different quantities; both can matter to the deployment decision.

Progress should be visible in people’s experience.

Language coverage remains worth expanding. The next question is what that coverage delivers: accurate answers for the intended task, understandable limitations, useful recourse, and evidence that the service works across the conditions it claims to support.

I want progress to mean that someone can ask in the language and form they use, receive an answer supported by current information, and get useful help when the system cannot answer. Limited resources make the design of that process more consequential. They should shape which evidence is collected, how the model learns, and how its limits are communicated.

Questions for linguists, practitioners, and language communities

I would welcome analyses of which distinctions these evaluation designs preserve and which they miss. For a particular service, I would begin with these questions:

  • Whose usage and communicative goals define an acceptable answer?
  • Which meanings, referents, or discourse functions are lost through transcription, normalization, translation, or generation?
  • When do reviewers identify a factual error, an unresolved interpretation, or an inappropriate response?
  • Who receives useful clarification and recourse, and who is repeatedly asked to accommodate the system?

Answers to these questions should influence the sampling plan, annotation rubric, learning objective, and deployment scope. Linguistic analysis belongs throughout that process.

How reliably does the system support a language, how is that known, and what happens when it fails?
